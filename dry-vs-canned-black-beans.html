Dry vs. Canned: The Real Cost-Per-Gram of Bulk Black Beans

A pound of dry black beans yields nearly six cans' worth of cooked beans once soaked and simmered. This audit walks through soak time, energy cost, and final price-per-serving — dry wins even after factoring in stove time.

Preparation Protocol

  1. Soak: Soak dry beans in water overnight, or at least 6 hours, then drain and rinse.
  2. Simmer: Combine soaked beans, onion, garlic, and bay leaf in a pot with fresh water to cover by 2 inches. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a low simmer.
  3. Cook: Simmer uncovered for 60-90 minutes, stirring occasionally, until beans are creamy and tender.
  4. Season and Finish: Remove the onion and bay leaf, stir in cumin, and season with salt to taste.

Why the Numbers Work

At $0.34 per serving, dry beans win the audit against canned even after accounting for the electricity or gas used during the roughly 90-minute simmer — a typical can of black beans runs $0.90-$1.20 for about 3.5 servings, or $0.26-$0.34 per serving before you even factor in that canned beans include added sodium and canning liquid you're paying for by weight. The gap widens further at true bulk pricing: buying dry beans in 5lb or 10lb bags instead of 1lb bags typically knocks another 15-20% off the $1.60/lb baseline used here.

Storage & Leftovers

Cooked beans keep refrigerated in their liquid for up to 5 days — storing them in their cooking liquid (rather than drained) keeps them from drying out. Freeze in portioned containers, liquid included, for up to 6 months; this is genuinely one of the best foods to batch-cook and freeze, since a single pot yields far more than one meal's worth.

Variations

  • No-soak method: Skipping the soak and simmering longer (2-2.5 hours) works if you're short on time, though the beans use more energy and can cook less evenly.
  • Add smoky depth: A dried chile or a small piece of smoked ham hock in the simmering liquid adds significant flavor for very little cost.
  • Instant Pot method: Pressure cooking un-soaked beans for about 35-40 minutes cuts the total time dramatically while keeping the cost advantage over canned.

FAQ

Do I really need to soak the beans first? Soaking shortens cook time and improves texture, but isn't strictly required — un-soaked beans just need roughly 30-60 extra minutes on the stove.

Why add salt at the end instead of the start? Salting too early can toughen the bean skins and extend cooking time; adding it once the beans are tender avoids that issue entirely.

Is the cost difference really worth the extra time? For batch cooking, yes — the time cost is mostly unattended simmering, and the beans freeze well, so it's an efficient trade for anyone meal-prepping.
